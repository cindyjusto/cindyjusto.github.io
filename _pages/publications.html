Working Papers

"From Nominal Access to Effective Competition: Task Heterogeneity and Price Formation in Digital Services Trade."

Abstract

Digital platforms provide buyers with access to a global pool of service suppliers. This paper examines whether costly entry, imperfect substitutability among suppliers and cross-border frictions limit the extent to which this access translates into effective competition in cross-border services trade. The analysis draws on winning and losing bids from a global online labour marketplace and shows that nominal access overstates the competition buyers face, as these forces operate at entry, in price formation and at the award stage. Bidder entry rises much less than proportionately with the qualified supplier pool and is lower where suppliers' skill portfolios are more dissimilar. Larger contests clear at only modestly lower prices, consistent with buyers selecting among more offers rather than with suppliers uniformly lowering their bids. Conditional on bid prices and track records, foreign suppliers are less likely to win, consistent with home bias, whereas prior engagement with the buyer raises the probability of selection. The results indicate that supplier substitutability, relational capital and cross-border frictions shape the division of gains from digital services trade.

"Learning from Giants? Complementarity, Competition and Services Export Adjustment under Trade Policy Shocks."

Abstract

Proximity to foreign multinationals may foster the transfer of knowledge and market information to domestic firms, but it may also expose them to competitive pressure and to the adjustment of the multinationals themselves. This paper examines which of these channels prevails when domestic services firms face trade policy shocks. Using UK firm-level data and exploiting the sequential trade policy shocks of Brexit, the analysis shows that the prevailing channel depends on the type of linkage. Production complementarity, which entails shared exposure to demand, sourcing and regulatory pressures, narrows the range of services exported, most markedly following the EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement, while firms raise the sophistication of the services they retain. Horizontal linkages reduce the probability of exporting, consistent with market stealing. By contrast, linkages that convey market information and know-how strengthen export performance. Market relatedness facilitates entry into new destinations, including more distant and sophisticated markets, and industrial relatedness raises export value. The results indicate that proximity to foreign multinationals does not uniformly enhance resilience, with implications for the domestic value of services FDI under trade policy frictions.

"Rules for the Intangible: How Digital Provisions in Trade Agreements Shape Services Trade."

Abstract

Digital technologies allow services to be delivered across borders, yet cross-border provision remains subject to national rules on data transfers, electronic transactions and consumer protection. This paper examines how digital commitments in preferential trade agreements and differences in domestic regulations shape bilateral services trade flows. Classifying digital rules by function and matching binding commitments to the domestic regulations they govern, the analysis shows that the relevance of digital commitments lies in their content rather than in their inclusion in an agreement. Services trade is higher where partners commit to maintaining regulatory standards, such as data and consumer protection, and lower where commitments limit the barriers governments may impose. Trade is also lower between partners with greater regulatory divergence. Binding commitments largely formalise regulations already in place rather than induce reform. The results indicate that the gains from digital trade rules rest on their substance and on regulatory similarity between partners.

"Heterogeneous Effects of Trade in Services Barriers: Firm-Level Evidence from the EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement."

Abstract

This paper studies the heterogeneous effects of trade in services barriers on firm-level exports, focusing on the consequences of the UK withdrawal from the European Union and the introduction of the EU–UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA). Using detailed firm-level transaction data on UK services exports combined with sectoral measures of regulatory restrictiveness, we show that UK services exports to the EU declined significantly following both the Brexit referendum and the implementation of the TCA, with substantially larger effects after regulatory barriers were formally reintroduced. The effects are highly heterogeneous across firms, following an inverted U-shaped pattern with small and large firms more negatively affected, and are particularly pronounced for foreign-owned firms using the UK as an export platform. Crucially, we show that barriers related to foreign entry are the primary drivers of the observed decline, while other regulatory dimensions play a limited role. These findings highlight the importance of firm heterogeneity and regulatory alignment in shaping services trade outcomes.
