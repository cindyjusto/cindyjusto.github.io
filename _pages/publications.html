"Learning from Giants? Complementarity, Competition and Services Export Adjustment under Trade Policy Shocks."

Abstract

This paper examines how foreign multinationals shape the trade performance of domestic firms. I construct firm-level measures of proximity between domestic services firms and foreign-owned services multinationals from their revealed trading behaviour, and exploit the sequential trade policy shocks of Brexit to examine how such proximity shaped export adjustment. Using UK firm level data, I find that proximity does not uniformly enhance resilience. Production complementarity amplifies the contraction, lowering export participation by 3.1 percentage points and export value by 21% per standard deviation of exposure, consistent with shared exposure to demand, sourcing, and regulatory pressures. The contraction is compositional: firms narrow their range of exported services while raising the sophistication of those retained, shifting toward more knowledge-intensive and, during the uncertainty period, digitally-intensive activities. This pattern is consistent with reallocation toward stronger capabilities rather than proportionate withdrawal. Market relatedness attenuates the shock, raising participation by 2.1 percentage points and entry into previously unserved destinations, including more distant and sophisticated markets, consistent with lower informational barriers. The findings suggest that the domestic value of services FDI depends on the nature of the linkage between foreign and domestic firms, and on how that linkage interacts with trade policy frictions.

"From Nominal Access to Effective Competition: Task Heterogeneity and Price Formation in Digital Services Trade."

Abstract

This paper examines how access to a global workforce translates into competition and prices in digital services trade. Using winning and losing bids from an online labour platform, I find that entry rises much less than proportionately with a project's qualified supplier pool and is lower where suppliers' skill portfolios are more dissimilar. Nominal access to global supply tends to overstate the competition a buyer faces. Where buyers solicit bids, thicker contests clear at lower prices. Buyers gain from selecting among more offers, with little evidence that suppliers uniformly shade their bids. Where buyers instead post a take-it-or-leave-it price, awards settle at that price, consistent with buyer price-setting power. Conditional on price and track record, foreign suppliers are less likely to win, consistent with home bias. Suppliers previously awarded a project by the buyer are substantially more likely to win. Neither the price response nor the foreign disadvantage, however, varies systematically with task complexity. A procurement framework rationalises how entry responds to the qualified pool and how prices respond to entry. The findings suggest that supplier substitutability, buyers' market power, relational capital, and cross-border frictions shape the division of gains from digital services trade.
