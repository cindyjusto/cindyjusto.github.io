Work in Progress

"Rules for the Intangible: How Digital Provisions Shape Services Trade."

Abstract

The digitalisation of services trade has been accompanied by increasingly complex domestic regulation and a proliferation of digital provisions in preferential trade agreements (PTAs), yet systematic evidence on how these regimes shape cross-border services trade remains limited. Combining bilateral services trade flows with detailed measures of digital policy in a gravity framework, this paper isolates the effects of regulatory divergence from those of PTA-based digital commitments, exploiting changes in digital policy while addressing policy endogeneity. Regulatory divergence depresses bilateral services trade, while digital provisions in PTAs facilitate it. Both effects are strongest for digitally enabled services but run through distinct margins: divergence operates mainly through intellectual property and payment system rules, whereas the impact of PTA provisions depends on their content — IPR provisions matter most for digitally and regulation-intensive services, while e-commerce and data-economy provisions matter more for traditional services. PTAs' trade-facilitating effects are largest where domestic institutions are weakest, suggesting international commitments can partly substitute for a weak regulatory environment. Digital services integration thus hinges both on the compatibility of national regulatory regimes and on the substantive rules governing digital trade within PTAs.

"Learning from Giants? Complementarity, Competition and Services Export Adjustment under Trade Policy Shocks."

Abstract

Foreign direct investment in services now exceeds that in manufacturing, yet evidence on how foreign multinationals shape the trade of domestic services firms remains scarce, reflecting in part the limited applicability of conventional MNE exposure measures to the services setting. Proximity to a foreign multinational can place a domestic firm in competition with it or in a complementary position. This paper constructs firm-level measures of proximity between domestic services firms and foreign-owned services multinationals (FSMs) from their revealed trading behaviour, and exploits the sequential trade policy shocks associated with Brexit to examine how such proximity shaped export adjustment. Using firm-level data from the UK, I find that proximity to FSMs does not uniformly enhance resilience. Production complementarity amplifies the contraction, lowering export participation by 3.1 percentage points and export value by 21% per standard deviation of exposure, a pattern consistent with shared exposure to demand, sourcing, and regulatory pressures across complementary activities. The contraction is compositional. Firms narrow their range of exported services while raising the sophistication of those retained, shifting toward more knowledge-intensive activities and, during the uncertainty period, toward digitally intensive ones. This pattern is consistent with reallocation toward activities in which they hold stronger capabilities rather than proportionate withdrawal. Market relatedness attenuates the shock, raising participation by 2.1 percentage points and entry into previously unserved destinations, including more distant and sophisticated markets, consistent with lower informational barriers. The findings suggest that the domestic value of services FDI depends on the nature of the linkage between foreign and domestic firms, and on how that linkage interacts with trade policy frictions.

"From Nominal Access to Effective Competition: Task Heterogeneity and Price Formation in Digital Services Trade."

Abstract

This paper examines how access to a global workforce translates into competition and prices in cross-border digital services trade. On an online labour marketplace, buyers post a project with a budget, suppliers bid a price, and the buyer chooses among the bids. Combining winning and losing bids with project specific measures of potential supply constructed from historical bidding records, I find that participation rises much less than proportionately with the size of the qualified pool and is lower where qualified suppliers hold more dissimilar skill portfolios. These relationships persist outside completed transactions, suggesting that nominal access to global supply does not translate proportionately into project-level competition. Furthermore, the relationship between participation and prices depends on the buyer's posting arrangement. When buyers invite offers through budget intervals, projects attracting more bidders are awarded at lower prices. Buyers gain from selecting among a wider set of offers, with little evidence that suppliers uniformly lower their bids. The participation-price relationship is weakest where suppliers are close substitutes. When buyers instead post a single price, awards closely track the posted figure, including when several suppliers bid. Foreign suppliers competing for the same project face a lower conditional award probability than domestic suppliers after adjustment for price and prior supplier history, consistent with home bias. A framework of project-level procurement, in which differentiated suppliers decide whether to enter and buyers choose among their offers, organises the analysis of participation, bidding, and selection. The findings highlight the role of supplier substitutability, relational capital, price-setting arrangements, and cross-border matching frictions in shaping how the gains from digital services trade are shared between buyers and workers.
